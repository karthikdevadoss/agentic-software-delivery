My AI evaluation system was less reliable than the AI it was evaluating

I built a multi-model verification engine to catch expensive wrong answers. In its final sprint the scorer carried nine defects and the engine under test carried two. Then the decisive result turned out to be an artifact of my own benchmark — so I stopped the project.

Status: experimental, paused. Product thesis: unproven.

This is an engineering and experiment-design case study, not a product announcement. It demonstrated zero genuine corrections of a wrong answer. Nothing here should be read as evidence that multiple models improve correctness.

The hypothesis, and why it was worth testing

Some wrong answers cost more than others. If a model tells you a PostgreSQL CREATE INDEX takes a SHARE lock when it takes ACCESS EXCLUSIVE, or that a Spring @Transactional self-invocation goes through the proxy when it doesn't, you don't find out from the answer. You find out from the incident.

The hypothesis was ordinary enough to be worth a real test: if two independent frontier model families answer the same question without seeing each other, their disagreements will concentrate where one of them is materially wrong. Catch the disagreement, challenge it, and you reduce published falsehood.

That is a measurement claim. It can be tested, and it can fail. Both of those turned out to matter more than the engine.

What I built

A bounded multi-model review behind a single tool interface. Two real subscription-backed model families answer sealed — neither sees the other, and the seal is proven against the real request text rather than inferred from call ordering. Zero paid API fallback, enforced in three independent layers and verified at zero paid calls across the entire programme.

Answers are decomposed into typed claims. Local code compares BOOLEAN, ENUM, NUMBER and VERSION deterministically, and never adjudicates free text — prose goes back to the models or is explicitly marked uncompared. Disagreement triggers one batched cross-family challenge under a hard four-call ceiling: no fifth call, and no synthesis model to paper over the gap.

Nothing is averaged, which was the design decision I'd defend hardest. UNRESOLVED, CONTRADICTED, UNCOMPARED and UNKNOWN are first-class outcomes. A system that always returns an answer cannot tell you when it doesn't have one, and that silence is the failure mode you can't audit.

How the evidence was frozen

This part is why the negative result is worth anything.

Ground truth and the decision rule were registered and hashed before any evaluated answer existed, behind a freeze that refuses re-registration. Every run retained its untruncated raw answers. Requested and reported model identity were recorded separately — and they genuinely differed twice, which is exactly the kind of thing you only notice if you store both. The final verdict replays offline from recorded turns with zero differences and zero model calls.

Freezing the decision rule in advance is what converts "we looked at the numbers and decided" into "the rule we committed to produced this". Without it, every result is negotiable after the fact, and the negotiation always finds a way to continue.

What the evaluator got wrong

As the controls got stronger, they kept finding defects — and increasingly the defects were in the measurement machinery, not in the thing being measured. Each was fixed with a test written and observed failing first.

Most of them could only create false disagreement, which is recoverable: it costs model turns, and the challenge round sorts it out. Two were worse. Two claims sharing a normalised subject collapsed into one routing entry, so a claim whose own two models both held their positions could be recorded as corrected-and-agreed while carrying a value copied from a different claim.

That is a silent false agreement. Nothing downstream would have questioned it. An assurance system whose failure mode is confident agreement is worse than no assurance system, because it manufactures exactly the signal you built it to trust.

The decisive result

The final sprint ran 20 pre-registered cases once each. The frozen pipeline mechanically emitted ONE MORE LOOK — the rule's way of saying the baseline had published material falsehoods the system caught.

That verdict rested entirely on two supposed baseline errors in a single case:

The baseline was right. My benchmark had pinned one spelling of a two-spelling value, and the ENUM comparator — correctly doing exact matching and never similarity — called it a conflict.

The comparator was not the bug and must not be loosened: exact ENUM equality is precisely what makes SHARE versus ACCESS EXCLUSIVE a real conflict rather than a near-miss. The defect was in the benchmark.

Why I stopped, and the four things I didn't do

The decision was pause. What makes that a real decision rather than a mood is the list of available moves I declined, because each one would have destroyed the result:

  • No next sprint. Starting one after a pre-registered pause converts the rule into a formality.
  • No ablation. A new experiment run in the shadow of a thesis that just failed is not a clean experiment.
  • No rerun. Re-running a clean completed experiment is the canonical way to manufacture a different outcome.
  • No replacement benchmark. The challenge set was selected by predeclared category before any model answered, and the baseline answered all twelve correctly. Building a harder set after seeing that is difficulty escalation, and it is how negative results quietly disappear from projects.

Both verdicts are preserved. The mechanical ONE MORE LOOK was never rewritten, because it is the only evidence the pipeline was followed mechanically rather than steered toward a conclusion. The human adjudication of that same frozen evidence sits beside it, labelled. Where the two disagree, the disagreement is itself the evidence — erasing it would remove the only proof the process was honest.

What was actually worth building

The engineering stands even though the product doesn't. Sealed independent answers proven against real request text. Typed claims with deterministic comparison strictly bounded to types where exactness means something. Explicit unresolved states with no forced convergence. A hard call ceiling honoured on every case. Offline replay that reproduces a verdict with zero model calls. Untruncated answer retention — which made one scoring correction free instead of requiring a second paid run. Mutation testing that killed 19 of 19. Evidence freezing that refuses re-registration. And provenance that recorded requested versus reported model identity separately, which caught a real discrepancy twice.

None of that validates the thesis. All of it is reusable.

What I'd do differently

  1. Score the frozen golden claims against a deliberately correct synthetic answer before freezing anything. One zero-cost control, no inference, no model calls — and it would have caught this project's decisive artifact before the freeze rather than after the run.
  2. Treat every assertion over prose inside control logic as suspect by default — in scorers, guards, routing and identity alike. That single pattern produced false conclusions five times across two sprints, in both directions.
  3. Budget evaluator hardening as a first-class workstream, not as overhead on top of the product. On the real numbers it was the larger risk.
  4. Choose cases where a strong model actually fails, without choosing them by watching it fail. My benchmark's baseline answered nearly everything correctly, so the experiment never gave the system a chance to demonstrate anything. That is a harder design problem than it sounds, and I underestimated it.

Reusable lessons

  1. Validate your evaluators as aggressively as you validate your models. Nine defects to two is not a close call. An assurance system that trusts its own scorer will publish the scorer's defects as product findings.
  2. Agreement between independent models is not external verification. Two models can be confidently wrong together, and your status vocabulary must not imply otherwise.
  3. Typed schemas reduce ambiguity; they do not eliminate semantic failure. An exact enum match cannot know that two spellings name one value.
  4. Measurement defects manufacture success and failure alike. One sprint produced a false regression, the next a false catch. Opposite directions — which rules out bias and leaves only "the instrument was unreliable".
  5. A negative result is only useful if the experiment is allowed to stop. The rule was frozen before any answer, and it was obeyed. That is the whole difference between a result and a story.

What this work does not show, stated plainly because a case study this detailed invites generous reading:

It does not show that this system beats any frontier model. It does not show that multiple models improve correctness. It demonstrated no FALSE → TRUE correction. It did not reduce hallucination. Consensus is not verification. It is not safer than a single model. And PAUSE must not be reinterpreted as "promising" or "partial success" — engineering quality and product validation are separate things, and only one of them survived here.

The part I keep coming back to

The harness found its own false positives in both directions, and the product stopped.

I spent six sprints building something to check whether models were telling the truth, and the hardest truth it surfaced was about my own measuring instrument. The instinct when a result goes against you is to question the result. The discipline is to have already written down what would count as an answer, and then to believe it.

That is the part I'd want to be judged on — not the engine.
